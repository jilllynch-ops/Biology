Characteristics of Life · Cell Structures · Levels of Organization · Specialized Cells

Lesson 1: Characteristics of Life

All organisms on Earth — from single-celled bacteria to complex animals and plants — share a set of basic characteristics and are built from the same basic unit: the cell.

Every living organism shares the following characteristics and abilities:

  • Cells — every organism is made of one or more cells, the basic building blocks of life.
  • Genetic information — every organism stores hereditary instructions (DNA / RNA) that are passed to offspring.
  • Obtaining and using energy — absorbing sunlight (like plants) or consuming other organisms (like animals).
  • Growing — single cells enlarge, and multicellular organisms add new cells.
  • Reproducing — passing genetic material to a new generation, sexually or asexually.
  • Responding to stimuli — sensing and reacting to changes in light, temperature, gravity, or other conditions.
  • Maintaining homeostasis — keeping internal conditions (temperature, pH, solute levels) stable.

Vocabulary

Organism — a living thing, such as an animal, plant, fungus, protist, or bacteria.

Cell — the smallest living unit of any organism; the basic building block of life.

Unicellular — an organism with a single cell that carries out all life functions.

Multicellular — an organism with many specialized cells that work together.

Homeostasis — the process of maintaining a stable internal environment.

Quick Check

A lizard basks in the sun until its body warms up, then moves to the shade once it gets too hot. Which two characteristics of life does this behavior illustrate?

Lesson 2: Cell Types and Cell Structures

Every cell — no matter how simple or complex — is built from a common set of parts, and every cell falls into one of two broad categories: prokaryotic or eukaryotic.

2.1 Every Cell's Core Components

Whether simple or complex, every cell shares four core components:

  • Plasma membrane — the outer boundary; separates the cell from its environment and controls what enters and exits.
  • Cytoplasm — the fluid (mostly water) that fills the cell's interior.
  • Genetic material (DNA / RNA) — nucleic acids that store hereditary information; genes are copied as RNA and carried to the ribosomes.
  • Ribosomes — the smallest structures in the cell; they decode genetic information and assemble amino acids into proteins.

Quick Check

A cell is found to have a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material, but no cell wall. Could this cell belong to an animal? Explain your reasoning on the lines below.

2.2 Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells

There are two main types of cells. Prokaryotes are unicellular organisms that lack membrane-bound organelles — their DNA sits directly in the cytoplasm instead of inside a nucleus. Prokaryotes are the oldest type of cell, originating about 3.5 billion years ago, and include bacteria and archaea.

Eukaryotes are more complex cells that contain a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. They originated about 1.5 billion years ago and include protists, fungi, plants, and animals. Eukaryotes may be unicellular or multicellular — but every multicellular organism is made of eukaryotic cells.

Vocabulary

Prokaryote — a unicellular organism that lacks a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

Eukaryote — an organism whose cells have a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

Organelle — a specialized structure within a cell that has a specific function (“little organ”).

Membrane-bound — enclosed within a membrane inside the cytoplasm.

2.3 Organelles of the Eukaryotic Cell

The eukaryotic cell contains a variety of membrane-bound organelles, each with a specific job:

  • Nucleus — stores the cell's DNA, packaged as chromosomes, and directs the synthesis of ribosomes and proteins.
  • Mitochondria — the “powerhouse of the cell”; synthesize the energy-rich ATP molecules cells need for life processes.
  • Rough endoplasmic reticulum (ER) — studded with ribosomes; builds proteins that will be transported outside the cell.
  • Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (ER) — has no ribosomes; builds fatty acids and lipids.
  • Golgi apparatus — receives vesicles from the ER, tags them with an “address,” and ships them where needed.
  • Vesicle — a small membrane sac that transports material within or out of the cell.
  • Cytoskeleton — a protein scaffold that maintains the cell's shape and structure.

The Protein Pathway

A useful way to remember how these organelles work together is to trace a protein's journey through the cell:

Nucleus→Rough ER→Vesicle→Golgi Apparatus→Plasma Membrane

Quick Check

Which organelle would be most abundant in a muscle cell, which needs a constant, high supply of energy? Explain.

2.4 Plant Cells vs. Animal Cells

Animal and plant cells share the same core organelles — but plant cells contain three additional structures that animal cells lack:

  • Chloroplast — captures the energy of sunlight and converts it into chemical energy (sugar). Contains the green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs light like a solar panel.
  • Cell wall — a rigid layer of cellulose surrounding the plasma membrane; supports and protects the cell and gives the plant its shape.
  • Central vacuole — a large sac that stores water, nutrients, and wastes. When full, it presses against the cell wall, keeping the plant firm and upright.

Vocabulary

Chloroplast — the plant organelle that carries out photosynthesis.

Chlorophyll — the green pigment that absorbs light energy in the chloroplast.

Cell wall — a rigid structure outside the plasma membrane, made of cellulose.

Central vacuole — a large organelle that stores water, nutrients, and wastes in a plant cell.

Lesson 3: Levels of Biological Organization

Every organism is a system built from parts that work together, organized into six levels, from simplest to most complex:

Organelle→Cell→Tissue→Organ→Organ System→Organism

Each level has a structure suited to its function, and each level is built from the ones below it:

  • Organelle — a specialized structure inside a cell (example: a mitochondrion).
  • Cell — the smallest living unit, built from organelles (example: a single muscle cell).
  • Tissue — a group of similar cells with a shared structure and function (example: cardiac muscle tissue).
  • Organ — two or more tissue types working together (example: the heart).
  • Organ system — a group of organs working toward a shared job (example: the circulatory system).
  • Organism — a complete living thing, built from one or more organ systems (example: a human being).

Vocabulary

Tissue — a group of similar cells that share a structure and function.

Organ — a structure made of two or more tissue types that performs a specific job.

Organ system — a group of organs that work together to perform a specific function.

Lesson 4: Specialized Cells and Body Systems

Structure and function go hand in hand at every level of organization. This lesson connects the shape of a cell, tissue, or organ to the specific job it performs.

4.1 Differentiated Cells and Tissues

The individual cells in a multicellular organism don't all do the same job. Instead, cells are differentiated — specialized to perform a particular function. A few examples:

  • Red blood cells — small, flexible disks shaped to squeeze through narrow blood vessels while carrying oxygen.
  • Nerve cells — have long, thin branches built to carry electrical and chemical signals across the body.
  • Muscle cells — long and fiber-like, built to contract (shorten) and produce movement.
  • Root hair cells — thin, hair-like extensions on plant root cells that dramatically increase surface area for absorbing water and minerals from soil.
  • Guard cells — paired, bean-shaped cells that open and close a leaf's stomata to control gas exchange and water loss.

A tissue is a group of similar cells that share a structure and function. The main animal tissue types are:

  • Muscle tissue — long, thin cells that contract (shorten) to allow movement.
  • Epithelial tissue — small, flat, interlocking cells that cover and protect body surfaces (for example, skin).
  • Connective tissue — holds organs in place and attaches epithelial tissue to other tissues (for example, bone, fat, and blood).
  • Nervous tissue — cells specialized to transmit electrical and chemical signals.

Vocabulary

Differentiated — specialized to perform a particular function within an organism.

Epithelial tissue — tissue that forms protective sheets covering or lining body parts.

Connective tissue — tissue that supports and links other tissues together.

4.2 Organs and Organ Systems

An organ is a structure made of two or more tissue types that work together to perform a specific job. The stomach, for example, is an organ: muscle tissue churns food, epithelial tissue secretes digestive acid and mucus, and blood (connective tissue) supplies oxygen to it all.

An organ system is a group of related organs that work together on a shared job. The human body has eleven major organ systems:

  • Nervous, Endocrine, Skeletal, Muscular, Integumentary (skin)
  • Immune, Circulatory, Respiratory, Digestive, Urinary, Reproductive

4.3 Interrelated Organ Systems

Organ systems rarely work alone — most rely on each other to meet the needs of every cell in the body.

  • Respiratory + Circulatory — the lungs bring oxygen into the blood and remove carbon dioxide (gas exchange); the circulatory system (heart, blood, vessels) then carries that oxygen to every cell in the body.
  • Muscular + Skeletal — muscles contract in opposing pairs, pulling on the bones they attach to and allowing the body to move.

Vocabulary

Gas exchange — the movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide between an organism and its environment.

4.4 Structure and Function in Plants

Plants are organized the same way animals are, but with plant-specific tissues. Vascular tissue is made of stacked, differentiated cells that form tube-like structures, allowing plants to transport water, food, and minerals — and to grow tall.

A plant leaf shows how structure fits function at every layer:

  • Wax cuticle — a waxy coating that keeps water from evaporating out of the leaf.
  • Upper epidermis — a transparent layer that lets light pass through to the cells below.
  • Mesophyll — the inner leaf cells where photosynthesis takes place.
  • Stomata — small openings on the underside of the leaf that let water vapor and gases move in and out; guard cells on either side control how open each stoma is.

Vocabulary

Vascular tissue — plant tissue that transports water, food, and minerals.

Root hair cell — a specialized root epidermal cell that increases surface area for absorbing water and minerals.

Guard cell — a paired cell that opens and closes a stoma.

Photosynthesis — using light energy to convert water and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen.

Stoma (pl. stomata) — an opening in a leaf that allows gas exchange, regulated by guard cells.

Quick Check

A leaf's upper surface is broad and flat rather than thick and narrow. How does this shape help the leaf carry out photosynthesis?

Central Dogma

Genes and Protein Synthesis

Lesson 5: DNA to Protein to Function

Here is one of the biggest ideas in biology: DNA → RNA → protein → cell function. DNA serves as a blueprint for building proteins, and those proteins carry out nearly every job a cell needs to do. This lesson covers the structure of DNA and the two-step process — transcription and translation — that turns a gene into a working protein.

3.1 DNA, Genes, and Chromosomes

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is a long chain of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains one of four nitrogen bases: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). DNA is double-stranded, and the two strands are held together by complementary base pairing: A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G.

The sequence of bases in DNA works like a code. Each group of three nucleotides, called a codon, stands for a specific amino acid. A region of DNA that codes for a protein is called a gene, and the rules for translating a DNA sequence into an amino acid sequence make up the genetic code.

Genes are organized on chromosomes. In eukaryotes, chromosomes are confined to the nucleus and are made of DNA wound around proteins called histones. Prokaryotic chromosomes are simpler, circular DNA structures located in the cytoplasm.

Vocabulary

DNA — the molecule that stores genetic information; a double strand of nucleotides.

Nucleotide — the subunit of DNA/RNA: a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen base.

Complementary base pairing — the rule that A pairs with T and C pairs with G.

Codon — a sequence of three nucleotide bases that codes for one amino acid.

Gene — a stretch of DNA that contains the instructions to make one protein.

Chromosome — a single piece of DNA, made up of many genes.

Quick Check

One strand of DNA has the base sequence ACGGTATCG. What is the base sequence of the complementary strand?

3.2 Transcription: From DNA to RNA

DNA never leaves the nucleus, so the cell first makes a working copy of a gene called RNA (ribonucleic acid). RNA is similar to DNA but is single-stranded and uses the base uracil (U) instead of thymine; uracil pairs with adenine, just as thymine does.

The process of copying a gene's DNA sequence into a strand of messenger RNA (mRNA) is called transcription. Special proteins first separate the two DNA strands. Then, an enzyme called RNA polymerase reads the transcribed strand and builds a complementary mRNA strand, one nucleotide at a time. In eukaryotes, the finished mRNA strand exits the nucleus through a pore in the nuclear membrane.

Vocabulary

RNA — a single-stranded nucleic acid that uses uracil (U) instead of thymine.

Transcription — the process of copying a gene's DNA sequence into mRNA.

RNA polymerase — the enzyme that builds an mRNA strand from a DNA template.

3.3 Translation: From RNA to Protein

Proteins are chains of amino acids — about 20 different kinds are used to build all proteins. The process of building a protein from an mRNA sequence is called translation. Translation happens at the ribosome, a structure made of rRNA and protein.

A second type of RNA, transfer RNA (tRNA), delivers the correct amino acid to the ribosome. One end of a tRNA molecule carries a specific amino acid; the other end has a three-base anticodon that is complementary to a codon on the mRNA. As the ribosome moves along the mRNA strand one codon at a time, each tRNA anticodon matches its codon, adds its amino acid to the growing chain, and is released. Special start and stop codons tell the ribosome where to begin and end.

Vocabulary

Translation — the process of building a protein from an mRNA sequence.

tRNA (transfer RNA) — the RNA molecule that carries an amino acid to the ribosome.

Anticodon — the three-base sequence on tRNA that is complementary to an mRNA codon.

Polypeptide — a chain of amino acids; another word for a protein.

Quick Check

A protein contains the amino acid sequence leucine-serine. If the tRNA anticodon for leucine is GAU and for serine is AGA, what was the mRNA codon sequence?

3.4 Protein Synthesis in the Cell

Together, transcription followed by translation make up the central dogma of biology: DNA is transcribed to RNA, which is then translated to protein. This process occurs in all living cells, both prokaryotic and eukaryotic.

In eukaryotic cells, several organelles work together depending on the protein's destination:

  • Proteins used inside the cell are translated by free ribosomes floating in the cytoplasm.
  • Proteins destined for the plasma membrane or for secretion are translated by ribosomes on the rough ER.
  • The rough ER modifies these proteins and packs them into vesicles, which travel to the Golgi apparatus for further modification.
  • The Golgi apparatus packages the finished proteins into vesicles that move to the plasma membrane, where the proteins are released by exocytosis.

Central Dogma pathway

DNA→transcription→mRNA→translation→Protein

Vocabulary

Central dogma of biology — DNA is transcribed to RNA, which is translated to protein.

Exocytosis — the fusion of a vesicle with the plasma membrane, releasing its contents from the cell.

Quick Check

Which organelle is NOT involved in the synthesis and secretion of a protein: ribosome, smooth ER, Golgi apparatus, or plasma membrane? Explain.

Lesson 6: Membranes of the Cell

The plasma membrane surrounds every cell, controlling what enters and exits. This lesson looks at the membrane's molecular structure and how that structure determines what can cross it.

6.1 The Phospholipid Bilayer

The plasma membrane is a semipermeable bilayer (two layers) of phospholipids. Each phospholipid has a polar, hydrophilic (water-attracting) “head” and two nonpolar, hydrophobic (water-avoiding) “tails.” The heads face outward into the watery cytoplasm and extracellular fluid, while the tails face each other in the center, avoiding water.

This structure determines what can cross the membrane: small, nonpolar molecules (like oxygen) pass through easily. Ions, polar molecules (like water), and large macromolecules cross slowly or not at all without help.

Vocabulary

Semipermeable — allowing some substances to cross more easily than others.

Phospholipid bilayer — the two-layer sheet of phospholipids that forms the plasma membrane.

Hydrophilic — “water-loving”; polar and attracted to water.

Hydrophobic — “water-fearing”; nonpolar and repelled by water.

6.2 Membrane Proteins

Water crosses the bilayer slowly, but it moves in and out of cells quickly thanks to membrane proteins. One type, called an aquaporin, is a protein channel shaped like a tube that lets water pass through easily. Membrane proteins are described by the fluid mosaic model: they float and move throughout the phospholipid bilayer, some spanning all the way across it.

Membrane proteins have several jobs: they stabilize and shape the membrane, act as “self-marker” identity tags recognized by the immune system, serve as receptors that bind hormones or other signaling molecules, and transport ions and polar molecules across the membrane.

Vocabulary

Aquaporin — a membrane protein channel that allows water to cross the membrane quickly.

Fluid mosaic model — the model describing membrane proteins moving fluidly within the phospholipid bilayer.

Receptor — a membrane protein that binds a specific hormone or molecule, triggering a response inside the cell.

Quick Check

Which should pass most easily through the plasma membrane: a protein, a small lipid, a sodium ion, or a water molecule? Explain.

Organelles inside the cell also have their own membranes. The nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, and Golgi apparatus are often considered one connected system, called the endomembrane system, that builds, modifies, and ships proteins and lipids within the cell.

Lesson 7: Passive Transport

Some materials cross the plasma membrane without the cell spending any energy. This lesson covers the three types of passive transport: diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and osmosis.

7.1 Concentration Gradients and Diffusion

When a dissolved substance is more concentrated in one area than an adjacent area, it forms a concentration gradient. Molecules naturally move from areas of higher concentration to areas of lower concentration — a process called diffusion — until the substance is evenly spread out, a state called equilibrium.

Diffusion requires no energy input; it happens automatically wherever a concentration gradient exists. Small, nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse directly across the phospholipid bilayer.

Vocabulary

Concentration gradient — a difference in the concentration of a substance between two areas.

Diffusion — the movement of molecules from high to low concentration; requires no energy.

Equilibrium — the state in which concentration is equal throughout a space.

Passive transport — movement of a substance across the membrane with no energy input.

7.2 Facilitated Diffusion

Some substances — like large molecules, ions, and polar molecules — cannot easily cross the phospholipid bilayer on their own. Facilitated diffusion uses transport proteins built into the membrane to help these substances move down their concentration gradient. Because the substance still moves from high to low concentration and no ATP is used, facilitated diffusion is still a form of passive transport.

  • Channel proteins — tube-shaped proteins (like aquaporins) that let specific substances flow through freely.
  • Carrier proteins — proteins that bind a molecule or ion on one side and release it on the other side of the membrane.

Quick Check

Glucose needs a carrier protein to enter most cells. Is this an example of passive or active transport? Explain your reasoning.

7.3 Osmosis and Tonicity

Osmosis is the diffusion of water: water moves from an area of higher water concentration (lower solute concentration) to an area of lower water concentration (higher solute concentration). Because a membrane may be permeable to water but not to a dissolved solute, osmosis can change the volume of fluid inside a cell.

Solutions are described relative to a cell's cytoplasm:

  • Hypotonic — lower solute concentration than the cell; water moves into the cell, which may swell or burst.
  • Isotonic — equal solute concentration; no net movement of water.
  • Hypertonic — higher solute concentration than the cell; water moves out of the cell, which may shrink.

Vocabulary

Osmosis — the diffusion of water across a membrane, from high to low water concentration.

Hypotonic / Isotonic / Hypertonic — terms comparing a solution's solute concentration to a cell's interior.

Lesson 8: Active Transport

When passive transport can't get the job done — for example, moving a substance against its concentration gradient — cells use active transport, which requires energy in the form of ATP.

8.1 Ion and Molecular Pumps

Membrane proteins called ion pumps and molecular pumps use ATP to move substances against their concentration gradient, from low to high concentration. A key example is the sodium-potassium pump, which uses ATP to move sodium ions out of the cell and potassium ions into the cell — both against their gradients. This creates concentration gradients nerve cells need in order to function.

Vocabulary

Active transport — movement of a substance against its concentration gradient, using ATP.

Ion pump / Molecular pump — a membrane protein that uses ATP to move ions or molecules against a gradient.

Quick Check

A cell has equal sodium concentrations inside and outside. Could passive transport create a sodium concentration gradient? Explain.

8.2 Endocytosis and Exocytosis

Some molecules are too large to fit through the membrane or a membrane protein, so the cell transports them inside vesicles instead.

  • Exocytosis — a vesicle moves to the plasma membrane and fuses with it, releasing its contents outside the cell. Used to secrete hormones and other large molecules.
  • Endocytosis — the plasma membrane pinches inward around material outside the cell, engulfing it in a new vesicle that moves into the cytoplasm.

Both endocytosis and exocytosis require ATP, making them forms of active transport.

Vocabulary

Exocytosis — a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, releasing its contents outside the cell.

Endocytosis — the plasma membrane engulfs outside material, forming a new vesicle.

Lesson 9: Homeostasis and Feedback Loops

Every organism performs a balancing act, regulating internal conditions like temperature, water, glucose, and oxygen within a narrow range. This process is called homeostasis, and it depends on feedback loops.

9.1 Negative and Positive Feedback Loops

A negative feedback loop is a monitoring-and-response cycle in which a change triggers a response that pushes the system back toward its original state — similar to a home thermostat. Most homeostatic mechanisms, including thermoregulation and blood glucose control, use negative feedback loops.

A positive feedback loop works the opposite way: a change triggers a response that amplifies the change, pushing the system further from its original state. Positive feedback loops are less common, and they typically drive a process to a clear endpoint rather than maintaining a steady state. For example, during childbirth, contractions of the uterus trigger the release of a hormone that causes even stronger contractions — a cycle that intensifies until birth occurs.

Vocabulary

Negative feedback loop — a mechanism in which a change triggers a response that returns the system to its original state.

Positive feedback loop — a mechanism in which a change triggers a response that amplifies the change.

Quick Check

Is blood clotting (where injured tissue releases chemicals that recruit more clotting cells, speeding up clot formation) an example of a negative or positive feedback loop? Explain.

9.2 Thermoregulation

The body maintains a stable internal temperature using a negative feedback loop. The hypothalamus, an area of the brain, senses blood temperature. If temperature rises too high, it triggers perspiration (which cools the skin as it evaporates) and dilation of skin capillaries (bringing blood, and its heat, closer to the surface to release it). If temperature drops too low, the body shivers (generating heat) and constricts skin capillaries (conserving heat).

Vocabulary

Homeostasis — the maintenance of a stable internal environment.

Thermoregulation — the regulation of body temperature.

9.3 Water Regulation and Transpiration

Organisms must also regulate the balance of water and solutes in their bodies (osmoregulation). Land animals conserve water by producing concentrated urine and by feeling thirst when water is needed. Freshwater fish, living in a hypotonic environment, excrete large amounts of dilute urine to eliminate the excess water constantly entering their bodies.

Plants regulate water balance through transpiration — the evaporation of water vapor out of a leaf's stomata. As water evaporates, it pulls more water up from the roots through the vascular tissue, like a straw. Guard cells open and close each stoma to balance the plant's need for gas exchange (open stomata) against the risk of losing too much water (closed stomata).

Gas exchange — taking in oxygen and removing carbon dioxide — also relies on passive transport (diffusion). In the lungs, capillaries surround microscopic air sacs called alveoli; oxygen diffuses into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses out, because each gas moves from an area of higher to lower concentration.

Vocabulary

Osmoregulation — the regulation of water and solute balance in the body.

Transpiration — the evaporation of water vapor from a plant's stomata, which helps pull water up from the roots.

Gas exchange — the diffusion of oxygen into and carbon dioxide out of the blood.

Alveoli — microscopic air sacs in the lungs surrounded by capillaries, where gas exchange occurs.

Quick Check

A jogger stops to drink water at a fountain. What homeostatic condition is this behavior helping to regulate?

9.4 Regulation of Blood Glucose

Blood glucose is regulated by a negative feedback loop involving two hormones from the pancreas. When blood glucose is high, the pancreas releases insulin, which causes cells to take in glucose and the liver to store it as glycogen. When blood glucose is low, the pancreas releases glucagon, which causes the liver to break down glycogen and release glucose into the blood.

Vocabulary

Hormone — a chemical signal released into the blood that affects tissues with matching receptors.

Insulin — a hormone that lowers blood glucose by increasing glucose uptake and glycogen storage.

Glucagon — a hormone that raises blood glucose by triggering glycogen breakdown.

Lesson 10: Looking Ahead — Mitosis & Differentiation

This unit closes with a preview of two ideas that connect everything you've learned so far to the next unit: how cells make more cells, and how those new cells become specialized.

10.1 Cells Divide for Growth and Repair

Every multicellular organism begins as a single cell. That cell divides into two cells, then four, then eight, and so on — building a whole body. Cell division continues throughout life, replacing worn-out cells and repairing damaged tissue. The type of cell division that produces two identical daughter cells, each with the same DNA as the parent cell, is called mitosis.

Vocabulary

Cell division — the process by which a cell splits into two new cells.

Mitosis — the type of cell division that produces two genetically identical daughter cells, used for growth and repair.

10.2 Specialized Cells Form Through Differentiation

A newly divided cell doesn't automatically look or act like a nerve cell, a red blood cell, or a root hair cell. Instead, cells become specialized through differentiation — the process by which a cell develops the specific structures it needs to carry out a particular job, even though it carries the exact same DNA as every other cell in the body.

You'll explore exactly how mitosis unfolds — and how differentiation is controlled at the genetic level — in the next unit.
